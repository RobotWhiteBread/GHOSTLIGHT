# GHOSTLIGHT Phase 2: entries for marking

Put an x in the box for each entry that lands. The ones that do not land are as useful as the ones that do.

## [ ] 1. The Whitcomb Place Fire Ball

*light, flint_hills, homestead, lightning_herd, ball_light, heat_lightning*

Among the old settlers it is *the spook light*.

It appears on nights of heat lightning with no thunder, as a ball of light that rolls along the ground and climbs the fences without slowing. A heat comes on the back of the neck like an open stove, sand drifts against the door when no wind blows, and fine ash settles on the sill. It keeps its ground and drives off those who linger there.

A homesteader named Hattie Sayre gave the first account in 1867. "It was round and it rolled, the way a wheel rolls, along the top of the grass." By the common understanding of the district, a cowhand named James Kimball was struck by lightning while riding night herd near the cattle pens at the siding on the Whitcomb place, and the cattle ran until morning.

Recommended precaution: The only rule is not to follow it, however near it seems or however familiar.

## [ ] 2. The Light at Pruitt Place

*light, high_plains, trail, frozen_blizzard, lantern, before_blizzard*

It is known to the old settlers as *the spook light*, and by that name it is recorded here.

Observers in the High Plains agree on its form: a lantern swinging at the height of a walking man, with no one carrying it. They agree also on the occasion, which is in the hour before a blizzard, and on the signs, which are that horses balk and will not go on and breath shows in a warm room. It is counted a friend. It leads the lost toward shelter and asks nothing for it.

The earliest account dates from 1853, when a bullwhacker named Samuel Kimball was caught by a blizzard between the barn and the house and was found frozen in the spring, not forty rods from the door. The first to report it was a laundress at the stage station named Hattie Doty, who said: "It was a lantern, plain as anything, at the height of a man's hand, and it swung the way a lantern swings in a walking hand."

A lamp burning in the window is courtesy enough; it is said to look for one. This is the counsel of the old settlers, and it has not been known to fail.

## [ ] 3. Brandt's Light

*revenant, high_plains, settled, lightning_herd, lantern, asks_the_way*

It is known to the German settlers as *der Wiedergänger*, and by that name it is recorded here.

Observers in the High Plains agree on its form: a lantern swinging at the height of a walking man, with no one carrying it. They agree also on the occasion, which is when someone is lost and asks the road, and on the signs, which are that there is a smell of scorched grass and the clock stops. It takes no notice of the living and seems only to grieve.

It is traced to 1906. A cowhand named Carl Kessler was struck by lightning while riding night herd near the water hole on the Republican River, and the cattle ran until morning. Within the year a telephone operator named Wilhelmina Rademacher reported the following: "It was a lantern, plain as anything, at the height of a man's hand, and it swung the way a lantern swings in a walking hand."

Set a stone on the grave each time you pass.

## [ ] 4. The Voice at Kjeldgaard Place

*revenant, platte, hard_winters, rail_accident, voice, threshing*

It is known to the Danish settlers as *the walker*, and by that name it is recorded here.

It appears while the threshing crews are in the district, as a voice that calls a single name, over and over, from somewhere out in the dark. There is a smell of scorched grass, sparks jump along the wire, and horses balk and will not go on. It neither helps nor harms. Those who have seen it close say it seems to be grieving.

A cowhand named Peder Kjeldgaard gave the first account in 1897. "It was calling a name. Not mine. I have never been able to forget the name." By the common understanding of the district, a section hand named Jens Rasmussen was struck by a night train at the bluffs above the river near the Kjeldgaard place while walking the track home from the section house.

Recommended precaution: Those who pass set a stone on the grave, and it lets them go by.

## [ ] 5. The Frenchman Creek Team

*revenant, high_plains, hard_winters, frozen_blizzard, phantom_team, before_blizzard*

Among the sheep men from the south it is *el ánima en pena*.

It suffers no one to stay long at Frenchman Creek, and those who try leave sooner than they meant to. It is a revenant of the High Plains, and shows itself as the sound of a team and wagon passing close by, though nothing is ever seen, most often in the hour before a blizzard. Its presence is known because the meadowlarks stop singing, horses balk and will not go on, and the dogs go silent.

The earliest account dates from 1887, when a cowhand named Tomás Trujillo set out ahead of a storm in the winter of 1887 and froze within sight of a lighted window that no one in it could see past the snow. The first to report it was a cowhand named Tomás Gallegos, who said: "We heard a wagon pull into the yard and stop. My father went out with the lantern and there were no tracks at all."

It asks for prayers. Say one for the soul and have a Mass said, and it will rest a while. This is the counsel of the sheep men from the south, and it has not been known to fail.

## [ ] 6. The Bone Rick at Heaney Place

*haunt, sandhills, hard_winters, bone_pile, singing_wire, windmill_stops*

The Irish section hands call it *the gentle place*.

It appears on the rare still nights when the mill stands idle, as wire that sings in still air, a long high note along the whole fence line. The dogs go silent, the grass lies flat in a path though nothing has passed, and there is a taste of iron in the mouth. It takes no notice of the living and seems only to grieve.

"You could hear it a quarter mile off, one long note, like a finger on a glass." So a hired hand named Timothy Dunleavy reported it in 1889. The explanation given locally is that the buffalo bones were gathered off the prairie for the bone trade in 1889 and stacked at the lake shore on the Heaney place for a train that did not come for them.

Do not answer it. Above all do not tell it your own name. Those who have neglected it have generally had cause to regret it.

## [ ] 7. Sondergaard's Crossing

*haunt, platte, settled, rail_accident, returns, wind_drops*

Among the Danish settlers it is *spøgelset*.

It appears when the wind drops at dusk, as an object that, taken away, is found again in its old place within the week. Horses balk and will not go on and sparks jump along the wire. It takes no notice of the living and seems only to grieve.

"My brother took it clear to town on the wagon. It was home before he was." So a farmer named Karen Sondergaard reported it in 1905. The explanation given locally is that a section hand named Peder Holst was struck by a night train at the old trail crossing on Buffalo Creek while walking the track home from the section house.

Recommended precaution: Set a stone on the grave each time you pass.

## [ ] 8. The Drought Voice

*spirit, sandhills, homestead, third_dry_summer, voice, wind_drops*

It is known to the Czech families as *hejkal*, and by that name it is recorded here.

Observers in the Nebraska Sandhills agree on its form: a voice that calls a single name, over and over, from somewhere out in the dark. They agree also on the occasion, which is in the stillness after the wind goes down at evening, and on the signs, which are that the lamp flame leans toward the door, the meadowlarks stop singing, and the grass lies flat in a path though nothing has passed. It neither helps nor harms. Those who have seen it close say it seems to be grieving.

The earliest account dates from 1877, when the Pecha family dated it from the third dry summer, when the wells went down and the wind never stopped. The first to report it was a homesteader named Marie Kovář, who said: "Somebody out there called my name, plain as day, three times. There was nobody for a mile."

Hang a wreath of herbs blessed on the Feast of the Assumption over the door.

## [ ] 9. The Bone Rick Fire Line

*spirit, dakota, homestead, bone_pile, fire_line, lamp_unlit*

It is known to the Bohemian settlers as *hejkal*, and by that name it is recorded here.

A spirit of place of the Dakota prairie, manifesting as a line of fire along the horizon that gives no smoke and does not spread. It is met with when the lamp is not lit at dusk. The coyotes hush all at once, the cattle drift and bunch against the fence, and horses balk and will not go on. It leads travelers astray, and the district has buried more than one who followed it.

It is traced to 1878. Kovář gathered buffalo bones for the bone trade and ricked them at the slough on the Kovář place, higher than a wagon, and left them there. Within the year a cowhand named Josef Puncochar reported the following: "The whole west was afire in a thin red line, and not a breath of smoke anywhere."

It will not cross an unbroken line of salt laid along the sill.

## [ ] 10. The Ahlers Place Heat Woman

*spirit, high_plains, homestead, prairie_fire, noon_woman, asks_the_way*

The local name, among the German families, is *the noon woman*.

A spirit of place of the High Plains, manifesting as a woman's shape standing in the shimmer of noon heat at the far end of a field. It is met with when someone is lost and asks the road. The dogs go silent and fine ash settles on the sill. It takes no notice of the living and seems only to grieve.

"She was made of the shimmer off the ground, and she had her hand up as if to call me in from the field." So a schoolteacher named Carl Ahlers reported it in 1879. The explanation given locally is that a schoolteacher named Louisa Brandt was burned in a prairie fire that came over the rise faster than a horse could run.

Recommended precaution: Those who pass set a stone on the grave, and it lets them go by.

## Notes from a first read

These are Claude's notes from reading an earlier batch of ten, before your marks. Use them as a starting point, not a verdict.

**What distinguished the ones that landed.** The signs echoed the death. A cowhand killed by lightning on night herd, appearing as a lantern, announced by a smell of scorched grass, reads as discovered: three traits point back at one event. The ones that fell flat leaned on the generic signs every haunting can have (the dogs go silent, the clock stops) and nothing tied them to how the thing began. In response, rule R14 now requires two signs that echo the origin or manifestation instead of one.

**The testimony carries the dread.** The dry survey frame works best when the quoted witness gets the one gothic line ("It was home before he was."). Entries where the template put the testimony first and the explanation after were the strongest of the four first-account forms.

**Fixed after reading:**
- A frozen searcher appeared as a dripping figure. Dripping now requires a water death.
- A section hand killed by a train appeared as a horseman. Railroad deaths no longer count as violence for riders.
- A Mass was offered for a rick of buffalo bones. Bone ricks no longer count as a human death.
- Czech *polednice* (the noon woman) was being used for every Czech spirit. It is now reserved for noon apparitions; other Czech spirits are the *hejkal*, which calls out to travelers. German noon apparitions are *die Mittagsfrau*.
- A haunt was named "The Schoolhouse School". Names that repeat a word root are now rejected.

**Still worth watching:** the precaution boilerplate ("This is the counsel of...", "Those who have neglected it...") will start to repeat once the guide has fifty entries. More variants there would pay off.
